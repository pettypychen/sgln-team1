# The 5% Zone (Adapted)

Adapted from Stephen Krempl's *The 5% Zone: Visibility Strategies to Get You Recognized and Rewarded in Any Organization*, as presented in the Global Leadership Communication Masterclass.

## The premise

> If you are not visible, you are invisible.

The larger the organisation, the easier it is to hide — and the scarcer the chances to be seen. In a global company you have many peers at your level, most of them competent, and the ones who get the next role are not reliably the smartest, the hardest-working or the most loyal. Those are table stakes. What separates people is whether senior leaders can say something specific about them.

Two consequences that matter more than they sound:

**It is not what they say in the room. It is what they say after you leave it.** "Great job, we need more people like you" is what gets said to your face. The conversation that decides anything happens after the call ends, and you are not in it.

**You are only as good as the person who came before you or after you.** Your work is not judged in isolation. It is judged against whoever presented in the slot on either side of yours.

## 95 / 5

Ninety-five per cent of the time, be yourself. That behaviour is fine and it is not up for discussion.

Five per cent of the time — specifically, in front of people two or more levels above you — you switch. Not because your normal self is wrong, but because at that level there is an expectation that you can state a point of view clearly, get to it without spending their time, and hold your ground when challenged.

This is not inauthenticity. You already behave differently with your team, with old friends, at a place of worship, watching your team play. You change constantly. The 5% is one more role, and it is the one that decides what you get to do next.

## The five situations

Senior leaders see or hear you in five places, and nowhere else:

1. **One-on-one meetings** — including skip-levels.
2. **Team meetings** — your function's, and the large town halls and all-hands.
3. **Conference calls** — where most people log on, listen and log off.
4. **Business presentations** — your slot, and the Q&A after it.
5. **Company socials** — dinners, receptions, the ten minutes before a call formally starts, the corridor.

The fifth is the one people dismiss, and it is the one senior leaders are most comfortable in. They can tell instantly whether you are comfortable there too.

Working remotely does not remove any of these. It compresses them.

## Four things to settle before anything else

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **95/5** | Decide that you are not changing who you are — only what you do in a handful of moments. |
| **Good vs Great** | The gap between good and great is a decision, not a skill. Most people stop because they have concluded they are already good. The relevant question is whether senior management thinks you are great, or just good like everyone else. |
| **Rules of the game** | Every organisation has written rules and unwritten ones. The unwritten ones decide outcomes. The specific question worth answering: *what are the rules of the game for your boss's boss?* That is usually the person who determines whether you are ready for the next level. |
| **Green X's** | Picture a flipchart: short red lines for the things that went wrong, green X's for the recent, specific, positive things senior people remember. Everyone has red lines. The question is whether you have enough green X's, and whether they are *recent*. Your job is to create them deliberately. |

## Romans are looking for other Romans

Headquarters is looking for leaders who look, sound and operate like the leaders already there. If you sit in a regional office and you do not sound like the people at HQ, they have no basis for handing you their legions.

This is not an instruction to imitate. It is an instruction to find out what HQ actually values, in what language it describes its own strong people, and to be legible in those terms rather than only in your region's terms.

## The response cycle

Three things decide how you show up before you open your mouth.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Image** | Self-concept → mindset → behaviour → results. Your results rarely exceed your self-concept. |
| **Intention** | Survive → Cruise → Contribute → Win. The bottom two are the defaults. |
| **Approach** | Be Prepared → Add Value → Be Different → Be Proactive. |

Intention is the one people misread in themselves. Under pressure the default drops to *survive* — get through it, do not get blamed. Survive is invisible.

## The little voice

You talk to yourself constantly. The constructive voice (LV1) keeps an open mind; the detrimental one (LV2) reaches for the file marked *why I can't*:

- "My work will speak for itself."
- "I'm an introvert, I'm not good at speaking up."
- "I don't have time."
- "They'll think I'm showing off."
- "I'm too new / too senior / too busy."
- "My situation is different."

Everyone has a *favourite* excuse — the one they reach for first. It is usually legitimate 95% of the time. The problem is using it 100% of the time.

The counter is not to silence it but to answer it: *"Thank you, that's a nice story, but now let's get on with it."* Then have a **cue** ready that flips the switch — a phrase, a physical action, a picture, a piece of music. Whatever reminds you: this is a 5% moment.

## The seven GEM behaviours

Krempl's Global Executive Mindset model — what the hundred executives he interviewed said they wanted more of:

1. **Articulates point of view clearly** — gets to the point, communicates at the level the room can act on, shows cultural sensitivity.
2. **Communicates confidently** — asks timely questions, interjects proactively, holds ground when challenged.
3. **Connects personally** — builds relationships at every level, leverages formal and informal moments.
4. **Gains trust** — delivers results, builds relationships, shows genuine concern.
5. **Provides direct feedback** — pushes back with appropriate assertiveness, shares bad news early, frames it carefully.
6. **Takes ownership** — shows initiative, readily leads projects, participates willingly. *This is the one leaders named most often as both most wanted and most lacking.*
7. **Coaches and recognises others** — guides and mentors, and praises openly.

You do not work on all seven. You pick the one or two that your 5% moments actually need.

## Saying the thing: POV, POC, TPV

**Set-up statements** buy you attention before your content arrives:

- "I have one point to make…"
- "There are three strategic imperatives I'd like to…"
- "I'd like to highlight a key idea…"
- "Great point, Kim — my take on that is…"

**POV** — your point of view, stated plainly.
**POC** — your point of concern, when you disagree: *interject, thank the person, then respond.* "I like the idea. Have you considered…" / "I see this slightly differently."
**TPV** — third-party validation, so the point is not just your opinion: *"The customer research shows three things…"*

**Keep it neutral or positive.** Your message lands as positive, neutral or negative. Being blunt and calling it authenticity is a trap — *authentically negative* is still negative, and senior leaders want to hear how the problem gets solved, not a catalogue of the problem. You are not avoiding the issue. You are choosing the framing.

**Questions count as much as statements.** In a Q&A, the question you ask is a 5% moment in its own right. One person asks "we tried this twice and it failed, why would it work now?" and another asks "I read that you rolled this out successfully with some global clients — how did you do it?" Same room, same information, very different outcomes six months later.

## Connecting: FORMING

For socials, corridors and the first minutes of a call. Ask broad, then narrow, and let them talk:

**F**amily · **O**rganisation/Occupation · **R**ecreation · **M**essage

**M** is the one people skip. Instead of "great speaking with you," give back one crisp line summarising what they were most animated about. It proves you were listening.

**ING** is what you have ready when they turn it around and ask what *you* are interested in: an **I**n-depth interest, a view on **N**ew technology or thought leadership, or a **G**lobal issue. "Mmm…" is a wasted 1% moment.

## Five techniques for the moment itself

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Increase your visibility** | Be in the right rooms and let people know you were there. Choose commitments deliberately — you cannot attend everything. Choose *who* deliberately: not the people you already know. |
| **Raise your energy** | Know your natural level and know how to get to 100% quickly, without your usual trigger. Most people have no idea how quiet they are on a call. |
| **Be different** | Do not repeat the person before you; "I agree" is not a contribution. But do not be a PIN — the person who comments on everything and is the only one who has not noticed the eye-rolling. |
| **Add value** | Bring something new. Come prepared. Note the difference between helping and handing someone more work: "you should redo that slide" is more work; "I can redo that slide" is help. |
| **Be proactive** | Volunteer before being asked, especially for the job no one wants — it is often the most visible one. When a leader asks for a volunteer, the first hand up is remembered even if it is not chosen. |

## The 1% moments

A handful of moments are worth more than all the rest: the unplanned lift ride with the CEO, the chance question at a town hall, the ten minutes at a dinner. They arrive without warning, which is exactly why they are prepared for in advance rather than in the moment.

Practise before you need it. The goalkeeper who saves the final penalty has dived to that side three hundred times a day.
